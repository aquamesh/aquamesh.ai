Example case study · Integrated refinery and petrochemical complex

$3.0M a year in the plant it already runs.

The plant

The site runs from crude and vacuum distillation through the hydrocracker, aromatics, the ethylene cracker and the polyethylene lines. Four cooling towers, the steam and boiler system and the water treatment plants serve all of it, and wastewater from every area ends up at one biological treatment plant whose air blowers keep its bacteria alive.

Most of what the audit found sits in those utilities. Three of the four towers waste water or chemical in different ways; one steam area sends too little condensate home; one boiler feed pump is wearing faster than its twins; one cooler is fouling quietly behind a valve.

What AquaMesh did

Read the historian. Model each signal's normal behaviour and compare every unit with its identical twin on the same site, then look for the units that stopped agreeing and the signals that stopped moving.

Then price it in code: every figure is a quantity × a unit cost × operating hours, with the basis written next to it. A line is labelled measured when the quantity was read off a tag, and estimated when it had to be worked out.

What it found

Nine causes, most of them in the utilities.

  • 5.3× vs 4.4×

    A conductivity probe that slid, and a tower that drains to match it

    Tower B has two conductivity probes on the same water. They agreed at the start and are now 20% apart. The water balance and the incoming water both say the second is right, so the probe the drain controller follows is reading about 20% off: the plant displays 5.3× reuse while the tower really reuses its water 4.4 times, and drains extra water to hold a number that is not real. A bench check settles it in a week — $252,879 a year.

  • 15 in 150 days

    Every polyethylene grade change flushes hydrocarbon to the sewer

    The line normally carries about 83 m³/h at about 116 mg/L of organic carbon. Fifteen times it jumped to 450 m³/h at up to 5,497 mg/L for about 6.7 hours — every one within three hours of a grade change, with the flush drain open each time. About 444 t/yr of hydrocarbon goes to the sewer ($271,687 a year), and each slug pulls dissolved oxygen in the aeration basins down to 0.2 mg/L and pushes effluent ammonia to 13.3 mg/L.

  • 38% → 98%

    A cooler fouling behind its own valve

    Cooler E4112’s heat-transfer ability is 37% below a clean cooler’s, while six comparable coolers held steady. The controller has hidden it by opening the cooling-water valve from 38% to 98%, so the outlet temperature stayed on target and no alarm fired. With the valve out of travel, the next stage is the outlet temperature climbing. It already pulls 432 m³/h more cooling water than a clean cooler would.

  • 63% vs 88%

    Condensate not coming home

    Steam gives up its heat in the process and should return as hot, clean condensate. Comparable areas return 88% of their heating steam; the ARO area returns 63%. At 76 t/h of steam, about 19 t/h goes to drain instead of back to the boilers, which must make it up from demineralised water, re-heat and treat it — $498,245 a year. The action is a steam-trap survey.

The other five are priced in the table below: tower A’s over-draining, tower C dosing inhibitor at a bad-week level, tower D’s throttled pumps, the fixed-air aeration blowers, and boiler feed pump 2 wearing against its twins. Two instrument leads — organic-carbon analyzers 13.3% apart and polyethylene export meters 1.0% apart — are flagged but kept out of the total.

What this is, and isn't

The historian is simulated, so the faults that were put into it are known. That allowed a test a real plant cannot offer: re-run the simulator with each fault removed, price the difference with the same unit costs, and set it beside what the audit reported from the data alone. The audit never saw that answer.

Seven of the nine causes came within 1% of it, and the total came to within 2%. A run on a healthy version of the plant, with no faults, finds $0. Those checks show the pricing logic is sound; they are not a claim about any operator, and the unit costs are assumptions to be replaced with a plant’s own.

The fine print

  • Raw water $0.95/m³, effluent $0.40/m³, electricity $0.048/kWh, demineralised water $2.1/m³, fuel gas $3.5/GJ, hydrocarbon lost to sewer $720/t, 8,059 operating hours a year — all assumptions, each with the range tested.
  • The wastewater effect of the polyethylene flushes is reported but not priced: the blowers do not follow load today, so the extra oxygen demand costs dissolved oxygen, not extra power.
  • Unit prices are assumptions, not a customer’s: replace them with the plant’s own and every figure rescales. The ranges show how far each total moves across the low and high price cases tested.
  • Savings are ceilings that assume each recommendation is carried out and then maintained.
  • AquaSpectra’s BOD reading is calibrated to a plant’s own lab results. Everything else it reads is change analysis, not a certified compliance method, and it does not replace any test a permit specifies.
