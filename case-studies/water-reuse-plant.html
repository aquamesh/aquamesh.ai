Case study · Membrane-bioreactor water reuse

25 days of history.
$34,870 a year in avoidable cost.

A water-reuse plant gave AquaMesh an export of its own historian — no new instruments, no site visit. What came back was a ranked list of what the plant was losing, what to check first, and what each item was worth over a year.

Process
Flat-sheet membrane bioreactor, recycled water for indoor reuse and irrigation
Scale
~140,000 gallons a day
Input
25 days of existing SCADA history, ~1,100 tags
New hardware
None

The plant

Wastewater arrives at a lift station, passes a fine screen, and runs through anoxic basins for denitrification into aerated pre-air basins. Membranes separate the treated water from the biomass; permeate pumps draw it through, chlorine contact disinfects it, and a reservoir holds it for reuse — indoor flushing, irrigation and fire suppression.

It is a small plant that does a demanding job: the water it makes goes back into an occupied building, so every disinfection and turbidity question carries real stakes.

What AquaMesh did

Read the export. Build a model of each signal's normal behaviour and of each pair of parallel units — two basins, two pumps, two membrane trains — then look for the pairs that stopped agreeing and the signals that stopped moving.

Every finding is priced. The plant's own energy tariff turns a wasted kilowatt into dollars a year, so the list comes back ranked by what it costs rather than by how unusual it looks.

What it found

Five things the plant could not see from its own screens.

  • 151,000 gal/day

    A potable line that never rests

    One line drew about 105 gpm around the clock and never dropped below 5 gpm in 25 days, while its neighbour varied normally through the day. A flat 24-hour draw is the signature of a continuous loss. Flagged for field investigation — if it is a leak, the pumping energy alone is roughly $8,000 a year.

  • 10.9×

    Twin basins that disagreed all month

    Two basins measuring the same variable read 5.14 and 0.52 mg/L of dissolved oxygen, with one blower pinned at 99.99%. A divergence that large and that persistent is a control fault, a split valve or a drifting instrument — and it held for the whole window rather than appearing as a transient.

  • 84% / 39%

    Pumps not sharing the load

    A lead/lag setting left one pump carrying more than twice the duty of its twin. The lead unit reaches overhaul years earlier than it needs to, while the standby sits idle — the same pattern showed up again on mixers.

  • 16 of 16

    Frozen reuse totalizers

    Every spray-field zone totalizer read exactly the same value for 25 days. Nobody could report how much recycled water each zone received, which is the number that reuse reporting is built on.

  • 8×

    Two turbidimeters, two answers

    The pair watching the same membrane filtrate disagreed eightfold at the 90th percentile. Whichever one is right, compliance decisions had been resting on a reading that could not be trusted.

In the software

Every finding says what to do and what it is worth.

The same findings as the operator sees them: severity, the action to take, the tags that are the evidence, and the annual value where there is one. Operators acknowledge, accept, resolve or dismiss — and accepted items are tracked to see whether the number actually moved.

$34,870/yr

identified in one pass

  • $18,490 energy
  • $16,380 reporting time

Where the value sits

Half of it is energy: air the process does not need, pumps running against each other, a scour setpoint the optimizer can lower without touching a single constraint. The other half is time — about seven hours a week of manual reporting that the plant's own data can produce on its own.

Neither number required new instruments. Both came out of data the plant had already been recording, and neither is realized until somebody acts on it. That is the point of the ranking: the list tells you which hour of work pays the most.
